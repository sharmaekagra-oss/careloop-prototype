User Test Protocol

A moderator's script for testing the CareLoop prototype with people who actually live with a chronic condition.

5–6 sessions ~40 minutes each In person preferred 2 people per session

1 Who to recruit

The prototype puts one bet at risk above all others: that a quiet app reads as trustworthy rather than broken. Whether that holds depends entirely on who is holding the phone. A 22-year-old will tell you the interface is clear. That is not the question.

The trap

Do not test with classmates. Every finding will be about usability and none will be about whether the design works for someone managing their own health. Three of the right people beat ten of the convenient ones.

Recruit 5–6 people, mostly from group A

GroupWhoHow many
A — Primary45+, taking daily medication for a long-term condition (BP, diabetes, thyroid, cholesterol, heart)3–4
B — CaregiverHelps a parent or spouse manage medicines, often from another city1–2
C — Gold dustAnyone who has stopped a course of medicine early because they felt better1 if you can find them

Parents, grandparents, aunts and uncles, neighbours, a family friend, the person at the local chemist's who knows their regulars. Group C is the direct test of the self-stopping design — Prof. Diwedi's parents are the archetype.

Screening questions

Ask before booking. Two minutes on the phone.

1Do you take any medicine every day, or most days?
2Roughly how long have you been taking it? (Want: 6 months or more — long enough for the routine to have gone stale)
3Do you use a smartphone yourself, or does someone usually help you with it? (Both are fine and useful — note which)
4Has there ever been a stretch where you stopped taking it, or missed it often? (Don't push; just note)

2 Before the session

Set-up checklist

  • Open the plain URL on the device the participant will hold — never the #facilitator one
  • Sound on, volume up. The check-in call speaks aloud and it is the centrepiece
  • Confirm the app is at the very first screen. If not: Settings → Start the test over
  • Test the voice once yourself on that exact device — voices differ by phone and some have no Indian English voice
  • Two of you: one moderates and says everything, one takes notes and says nothing
  • Phone in their hands, not yours. Resist reaching for it

Roles

RoleDoes
ModeratorReads the script, sets tasks, asks probes, controls the simulation rail when the script says to
Note-takerFills the capture sheet, writes verbatim quotes, records timestamps of hesitation. Does not talk, does not help
Consent — say this, out loud, every time

“Before we start — this is a class project, not a real medical product. I'll be taking notes on what you do and say, but not your name or anything about your own health records. Nothing is recorded unless you say it's alright. You can stop at any point, and you can skip any question. Is that alright with you?”

If recording audio, ask separately and get a clear yes.

3 Warm-up — 5 minutes

Do this before they see the app. You are capturing their real current behaviour, which becomes the yardstick for everything after.

Say

“Before I show you anything — tell me about your medicines. What do you take, and when?”

Then
  • “Walk me through yesterday. When did you take it?”
  • “What helps you remember?” (pill box, family, habit, alarm, nothing)
  • “When did you last miss one? What happened?”
  • “Does anyone else keep an eye on it?”
  • “Have you ever stopped taking something before the doctor said to?” — the key one; ask gently, don't judge
Write down

Their exact words for the medicines. Who they name as a helper. Whether missing a dose registers as a real event or a shrug. You will reuse all of this in the probes later.

4 The tasks

Rules for the whole section
  • Do not demo. Hand it over and let them drive.
  • Do not explain. If they are confused, that is the finding — write it down instead of rescuing them. Wait a full ten seconds before helping.
  • Do not say “tiers”, “green”, “red”, or “AI” until they do. Whether the system explains itself is precisely what you are testing.
  • Never ask “would you use this?” Everyone says yes. It tells you nothing.
Hand-over — say

“This is a rough prototype, so some parts are half-built. You can't break it, and if something is confusing that's the app's fault, not yours — those are exactly the bits I need to hear about. Please think aloud as you go: tell me what you're looking at and what you expect to happen.”

1

Getting set up

Onboarding · does the consent screen register?

6 min
Say

“Imagine you've just come out of an appointment. The clinic has set this up for you on your phone. Go ahead and get yourself set up.”

Watch for
  • Do they read the voice-choice screen or tap past it? Do they play the sample?
  • At the sharing screen — do they notice the toggles at all? Do they change any?
  • Any hesitation about naming a family member
Probe after
  • “What did that last screen say would be shared?” — tests whether consent was understood or clicked through
  • “Who would you actually put down there, in real life?”
2

A normal day

The silence bet — the single riskiest assumption

7 min
Say

“Now you're at home and the day is starting. Use it as you normally would. I'll move the clock forward for you.”

Advance with Skip to next moment. Let them mark doses. Keep them on track so the app stays quiet — that is the point of this task.

Watch for
  • Do they notice that nothing notified them?
  • Do they open the app unprompted, or wait to be told?
  • Does the status panel at the top get read, or ignored as decoration?
Probe after — the money questions
  • “Did the app remind you to take anything today?”
  • “It stayed quiet the whole morning. How does that sit with you?”
  • “Would you have wanted it to say something? What, and when?”
  • “If it stayed that quiet for a week, what would you think was going on?” — listens for “it's broken” vs “it trusts me”
3

The call

Care or surveillance? Tests the caregiver-boundary assumption

8 min
Say

“This time, you've had a busy morning and haven't got to your tablets. Just carry on without taking them.”

Advance until the gentle nudge appears, then keep going until the call comes. Let the voice finish speaking — do not talk over it, however awkward the pause feels.

Watch for
  • Their face and body the moment the call arrives. Note it before they say anything
  • Do they realise it is automated? Do they say so unprompted?
  • Which button they reach for, and how long they take
Probe — hardest questions in the session
  • “Tell me what just happened.”
  • “How did that feel?” — then stay silent. Let them fill it
  • “Who did you think was calling?”
  • “Would you want that call at home? What about if someone else was in the room?”
  • “Is there a time of day that would be wrong for this?”
  • Then let them answer No, not yet, and ask: “Your daughter has now been told. How do you feel about that?”
Careful

This is where the design is most likely to fail, and where people are most likely to be polite about it. Push past the first “it's nice.” Ask “what would your husband have said if he'd heard it?”

4

“I feel fine now”

The self-stopping path — does an explanation beat a reminder?

7 min
Say

“Now imagine a few weeks have gone by. You feel completely well — no symptoms at all. You're starting to wonder whether you still need these tablets. Show me what you'd do.”

If they don't find it, steer only as far as: “what would you tap if you weren't going to take it?” Then let them choose their own reason.

Watch for
  • Which reason they pick without prompting
  • Do they read the explanation, or skim and dismiss?
  • Do they use “Hear this”?
  • Which button they end on — and how long they hover first
Probe after
  • “In your own words, what did that just tell you?” — tests comprehension, not satisfaction
  • “Did that change your mind, honestly?”
  • “Has anything like this happened to you or someone in your family?”
  • “Who would you actually believe about this — an app, your doctor, or your family?”
5

The comparison

Counterbalance the order — see the note below

6 min

Reset the app, switch the mode in the simulation rail, and replay the same day in the other style.

Say

“Here's a different version of the same app, on the same day. Same tablets, same times. Tell me what you notice.”

Alternate the order between participants

Participants 1, 3, 5 → quiet version first. Participants 2, 4, 6 → the noisy one first. Whichever comes second tends to win on novelty alone; alternating cancels that out. Write down which order you used.

Probe after
  • “What was different between the two?”
  • “Which one would you want on your own phone? Why?”
  • “Think a month ahead, not today. Which one are you still opening?”
  • “Was there a point where the second one started to annoy you? When?”
6

Running out

Refill and records — lighter, only if time allows

4 min
Say

“You want to check whether you have enough tablets to last the week. Have a look.”

Probe
  • “How do you keep track of running out at the moment?”
  • “When would you want to be told — how many days before?”
  • Then, on the health file: “Your doctor asks for last month's report. Show me what you'd do.”
  • “Would you be comfortable sharing that with a chemist? With a doctor you'd never met?”

5 Closing — 4 minutes

Ask, in this order
1“What's the one thing you'd change?”
2“Was there any moment that felt uncomfortable, or like too much?”
3“Who else in your family would this be for — you, or someone you look after?”
4“If your clinic handed you this tomorrow, what would you want to ask them first?”
5“Anything you expected to find and didn't?”
Close with

“That's everything — this was genuinely useful. Thank you.”

7 What each task is actually testing

Keep this out of sight during the session. Use it when you synthesise — and put it in the writeup, because it shows the tests were designed against your assumptions rather than fitted to the results afterwards.

8 After each session, and after all of them

Within ten minutes of the participant leaving

Memory decays faster than you think. The two of you, out loud, five minutes:

  • What surprised us?
  • What did they do that contradicted what they said?
  • One sentence: what would this person want us to change?

Once all sessions are done

  • One observation per sticky note, participant number on each. Cluster by what happened, not by feature
  • A pattern needs three or more participants. Two is a hunch. One is a story — useful for the writeup, not for a decision
  • Split findings into the design is wrong versus the prototype is rough. Only the first should change the concept
  • Update the evidence-vs-assumption table: move what you confirmed into evidence, and be honest about what stayed an assumption
Watch yourselves for
  • Counting polite enthusiasm as validation. “This is nice” is not a finding
  • Explaining away the moments people struggled — those are the results
  • Only recording what supports the design you already built
